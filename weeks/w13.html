W13: Dynamic Memory and Recursion

Dates: Mon Oct 26 & Tue Oct 27, 2026 · 8:00–8:50 am · 12LH3004

Syllabus module: 5 — dynamic memory allocation; functions and recursion

Plan

Monday — dynamic memory allocation. Every array you have declared so far had its size fixed when the program was compiled. That is a real limit: you cannot read a list whose length you only discover while running. malloc asks for memory while the program runs and returns its address; free gives it back. The stack and the heap as two different places, why malloc can fail and what it returns when it does, and the three mistakes that follow: leaking memory, using it after freeing it, and freeing it twice.

Tuesday — recursion. A function that calls itself. The base case that stops it, the recursive case that makes progress towards the base case, and what the call stack looks like while it runs — which is easier to picture now that Monday has separated the stack from the heap.

Pre-read

  • Lec 19 · Lec 20 · Lec 24
  • The W10 notes on pointers. malloc returns an address, so everything this week rests on that.

PAGAL Notes

PAGAL — Perspectives on Algorithms, Generative AI & Learning: the week’s informal column.

Next door in Jagti, they were discussing your job.

On 9–10 October, on the IIM Jammu campus in Jagti — the same village as this institute — there was a conference called the J&K GCC Summit 2026, organised with the Government of Jammu & Kashmir under the theme “Unlocking J&K’s GCC Potential for Building Growth Ecosystem & Catalysing Investment.” Over 300 delegates. The Lieutenant Governor gave the inaugural address and the Chief Minister the valedictory. Six panels over two days, two of which were about you:

Panel 4 — GCC Innovation and Technology: Unlocking Tier-2 and Tier-3 Advantage

Panel 5 — The Future of GCC Talent and Workforce Transformation

A Global Capability Centre is what used to be dismissed as a “back office” and no longer is. It is a centre a multinational owns and staffs itself, increasingly doing the engineering rather than supporting it. The numbers the summit put up:

GCCs operating in India 2,117+
Professionals employed 1.9 million+
GCCs in Tier-II and Tier-III cities 220+
Projected workforce growth by 2030 39%
Share of the future workforce from non-metro locations 84%

(Figures as presented by the summit, sourced to Zinnov, NASSCOM, EY, BCG and McKinsey.)

Read the last row again, because it is the one that concerns this classroom. The argument of the entire summit was that this work is moving to places like Jammu — and that the binding constraint is not real estate or policy or bandwidth. It is whether there are enough people here who can do the work.

That is an unusually direct thing for a first-year programming class to be told. The question of whether such a centre opens in this city, and whether it does serious engineering when it does, is partly a question about the people sitting in this room.

What the industry leaders actually emphasised.

The programme is on the website; the emphasis is not. What came back from those panels was consistent, and it was not about any particular technology.

Continuous learning, as a condition of the job rather than a virtue. Not “keep learning” as a motivational poster. The stronger claim: that the specific tools you are hired for will be substantially replaced during your employment, so the only durable qualification is the demonstrated ability to pick up the next thing quickly. What you know is a depreciating asset. How fast you learn is not.

Compressing onboarding. This one is measurable, which is why industry cares about it. How long before a new engineer can safely change something? It used to be months. The pressure now is to make it weeks. And notice what that is actually a demand for: it is a demand that you can be dropped into a large unfamiliar codebase and become useful quickly. That is precisely the skill last week’s column was about, and which Google now runs an interview round on. Two completely different institutions — a hiring process in California, a talent panel in Jagti — arriving at the same requirement in the same month.

Judgement early, not after a decade. The expectation voiced repeatedly was that juniors will move from producing work to judging work far sooner than before, because the production is increasingly done by AI. The junior role is shifting from “write this” to “decide whether this is right, and whether it is the right thing.”

If that sounds familiar, it should. Week 1 of this course drew it as Specify → Express → Verify and claimed the machine was taking the middle step. That was our argument. It is now also the argument of a room full of people who hire engineers. We did not get that from them and they did not get it from us; it is simply what the situation looks like from both ends.

The part that was not about skills at all.

Here is what genuinely stood out. In a summit about technology, investment and AI, the talent discussion kept returning to things that are not skills: grit, trustworthiness, ownership, the willingness to say you do not know.

That is easy to dismiss as the soft part of a corporate panel. It is worth taking seriously instead, because there is a reason it is getting more weight rather than less.

When producing code was the bottleneck, you could measure someone by output. That measurement is now broken: anyone can produce plausible output. So what remains to evaluate a person on? Whether their work can be relied upon. Whether they say “I checked this” only when they checked it. Whether they stay with a hard problem past the point where it stops being interesting. Whether, when they do not understand something, they say so instead of shipping it and hoping.

The skill landscape moves every few years. These do not. They are also, inconveniently, the ones you cannot acquire the night before.

Which brings us, honestly, to malloc and free.

This is not a stretch, and it is why this column sits in this particular week.

free is a promise that nobody checks. If you forget it, your program still compiles. It still produces the right answer. It passes the tests. It passes code review, most of the time. The leak shows up weeks later as a service that degrades overnight, and by then it is somebody else’s night.

There is no compiler error for a broken promise. C gives you the memory because you asked, and it believes you when you imply you will give it back. The language has no opinion about whether you do. Nothing in this course so far has worked that way: a missing semicolon is caught instantly, a wrong answer is caught by the grader. A leak is the first thing you will write that is wrong in a way that only hurts someone who is not you, and only later.

Now separate two things the word trustworthy is doing, because they are not the same and the connection between them is the whole point.

What it means Where it lives
A trustworthy work product the program does what it claims, including the parts nobody inspected — it frees what it allocated, it handles the file that would not open in the artefact
A trustworthy person colleagues can rely on what you hand them and on what you say about it, without re-checking everything themselves in you

free is the first kind. That is a fact about your code.

The panellists in Jagti were arguing about the second kind. That is a claim about a person, and it is the one being hired for — because, as above, output is no longer evidence of anything.

The connection runs one way, and it is worth being exact about the direction. Nobody becomes trustworthy by resolving to be trustworthy. You become trustworthy by producing trustworthy work, enough times, that people stop needing to verify it. The trait is the accumulated residue of the artefacts. It is built out of instances — this free that you did not skip, this edge case you checked before claiming it worked, this time you said “I am not sure” when the easier thing was to say nothing.

Which is why a memory leak is a slightly larger matter than it appears. It is not a character flaw; it is a bug. But the habit that prevents it — finishing the part nobody will inspect — is the same habit that, repeated for a few years, is what people mean when they call someone reliable. You do not get to build that later. You are building it now, or not, in whichever way you handle the free that nobody is going to check.

Recursion, on Tuesday, asks something related. To write a recursive function you must trust the call you are making — assume it returns the right answer for the smaller problem — while remaining entirely responsible for the base case and for making progress towards it. Trust the part you delegate; own the part that makes it terminate. That is also, more or less, a description of working with an AI.

What to do about it, concretely.

The summit’s message to this region was that the work is coming here if the people are here. Three things follow, none of them dramatic:

  1. Get fast at reading code you did not write. It is the measurable thing industry is now optimising for, under the name “onboarding.” The drills are practice at it.
  2. Protect the ability to judge. Use every tool you like — but stay able to say why an answer is right, because that is the part being hired for.
  3. Build the trait out of the artefacts. free what you malloc. Say when you do not know. Finish the hard one. Each is a small piece of work that holds up unobserved, and that is the only material the reputation is ever made of.

The third is the one that will feel least like computer science and will matter longest.

Tuesday, 27 October: Even the arithmetic underneath AI needs debugging

Remember the Pentium division bug: Thomas Nicely noticed that a trusted processor gave inconsistent answers. Remember Java’s binary-search bug: (low + high) / 2 could overflow before the division happened. A mathematically sensible expression failed on a machine with finite integers. Humans investigated both. Joshua Bloch’s account

Now consider softmax, which converts scores into probabilities inside many AI systems. Barron and colleagues describe two numerical bugs in standard softmax and cross-entropy implementations. Subtracting the largest input already guards against exponential overflow, but rounding can still erase a small learning signal. Another failure produces a non-zero learning signal even when a student model’s scores exactly match its teacher’s. Paper and demonstration

Here is the numerical trap in miniature. For a tiny positive \(u\),

\[ \frac{1}{1+u}-1 = -\frac{u}{1+u}. \]

On paper, they are equal. On a computer, 1 + u may round to exactly 1. The first expression then becomes zero; the second can preserve the small negative value.

This is loss of precision, occurring before \(u\) itself becomes too small to represent. Overflow exceeds the available range; underflow concerns values near the bottom of that range. Avoiding those extremes does not guarantee accurate arithmetic. The paper’s fixes rearrange computations to preserve information that the usual formulas discard. Technical explanation

The reported Gemini assistance adds a striking possibility: AI helping humans repair the numerical machinery used to train AI. That attribution remains unverified here: the paper does not mention Gemini, and we could not independently verify the details in the linked discussion. The collaboration we should aim for is clear: ask the model to challenge an implementation, propose counterexamples and compare alternative formulas. Then reproduce the failure and test the proposed repair.

For a first-year programmer, this is why representation, precision and boundary cases matter. They reach all the way from your C exercises to modern AI. You need enough understanding to recognise a useful diagnosis—and enough discipline to demand evidence before shipping the fix.
