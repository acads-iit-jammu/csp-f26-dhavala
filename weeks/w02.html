W02: The Computer, Revisited · Number Systems

Dates: Mon Aug 10 & Tue Aug 11, 2026 · 8:00–8:50 am · 12LH3004

Syllabus module: 1 wrap-up (computer hardware & software) · 2 — Number systems, conversions, and arithmetic

Pre-read

  • Last week’s take-home question: why does 72 mean H? Codes are conventions — this week we go one level deeper: how does 72 itself live in a machine?
  • Skim Lec 3 — The Computing Machine (Prof. Shweta Agrawal, IIT Madras): computer organization, bits and bytes, and first contact with binary.

In Class

Monday — the computer, revisited (and programmed):

  • 📽️ Deck 04 — The Computer, Revisited — the recap that closes Week 1’s loop: what the Turtle taught (programming) and what the Display machine taught (the stored procedure), the design’s proper name — von Neumann architecture — and the discussion trap: the Toy Computer adds and subtracts… so isn’t it just a calculator? (The dividing line is where the procedure lives.)

  • 🧮 The Toy Computer — a complete von Neumann machine you can see all of at once: 40 cells (instructions tinted blue, data green — the machine can’t tell), ACC and PC on display, ten instructions, and a diary narrating every fetch–decode–execute. Work the six-rung ladder: one problem (adding two integers), one new idea per rung, ending at the recursion wall.

  • 🔧 Under the Hood (a guided climb, in three parts: The Parts · Memory & Time · The Machine) — and what is the Toy Computer made of? Build every floor yourself: a switch flipped by a wire → gates → an adder → a flip-flop (from two NOR gates and a loop) → the clock → a complete flip-flop machine with a timing diagram. One precision to savour: Part 3’s machine is honestly built from flip-flops but its plan is frozen in its wiring — by Monday’s own dividing line, it is a calculator. The Toy Computer is the one with the stored program. The pair of them, side by side, are the lesson.

Tuesday — number systems:

  • 📽️ Deck 03 — Number Systems — the week’s slides:
    • Radix-q positional systems — one idea behind every numeral (value = digits × powers of q); tally marks (q=1) to ternary to the popular costumes: decimal (q=10, courtesy of anatomy) and hexadecimal (q=16, binary’s shorthand). Binary (q=2) is what computers wear — because switches have two reliable states.
    • Conversions, both directions: binary→decimal by weights, decimal→binary by repeated division; hex ↔︎ binary four bits at a time.
    • Negative integers — there is no minus sign in a cell: sign-magnitude, one’s complement, and the winner, two’s complement (one adder does everything).
    • Real numbers — fixed point (the stuck ruler) and floating point (binary scientific notation), ending at the world standard: IEEE FP32 — 1 sign + 8 exponent (bias 127) + 23 fraction bits, worked example and the 0.1 != 0.1 warning.
  • 🎡 The Number Playground — three interactive machines for this week: toggle 8 bits and watch the weights sum; spin the complement sundial (the same pattern read by four conventions — find the overflow cliff at 0111→1000, and the double zeros); and dissect IEEE FP32 live — flip an exponent bit and watch the value leap worlds.
  • Lec 3 — companion slides (Prof. Shweta Agrawal, IIT Madras)

After Class

  • Toy Computer: climb rungs 1–4 yourself; predict every step before pressing it. Brave? Run rung 6 and explain to a friend why the answer is stranded in memory.
  • Convert 100, 255, and your roll number to binary and hexadecimal; write −77 in 8-bit two’s complement; decode the FP32 pattern 0 10000010 01000000000000000000000.
  • Verify one of your conversions the machine’s way: does 42 + (−42) really give all zeros in 8 bits?
  • A terminal experiment: in Python, type 0.1 (prints 0.1 — looks exact!), then 0.1 + 0.2, then format(0.1, '.20f'). Reconcile what you see with the FP32 slide — the resolution is in Deck 03 and the Playground.

PAGAL Notes

PAGAL — Perspectives on Algorithms, Generative AI & Learning: the week’s informal column.

How many bits does a number deserve? (FP32 → FP16 → NVFP4)

This week you met IEEE FP32 — the 32-bit costume the world agreed real numbers should wear: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, 23 fraction bits, about seven trustworthy decimal digits. For thirty years that was simply what “a real number” meant in a computer. Then the AI industry asked a heretical question: who says a number deserves 32 bits?

The first cut was gentle. FP16 — “half precision” — keeps the same three-part anatomy but on a diet: 1 sign, 5 exponent, 10 fraction bits. Half the memory, twice the numbers per second through the same hardware, and for neural networks it mostly… worked. That “mostly” is the interesting part: a neural network is not a bank ledger. It is a huge fabric of approximate quantities whose collective pattern matters far more than any individual value’s seventh decimal digit. Precision, it turns out, was being spent where nobody was reading it — and this week’s deepest idea (meaning lives in the reader) applies to precision too.

So the diet continued: 8 bits, then four. NVIDIA’s current answer, introduced with its Blackwell chips, is NVFP4 (the official name — often misheard as “NFP4”). A single NVFP4 value is four bits in E2M1 anatomy: 1 sign, 2 exponent, 1 fraction bit. Stop and let that register against Deck 03: two exponent bits and one fraction bit can express only sixteen distinct values, with magnitudes no larger than ±6. As a stand-alone number format, that is almost comically poor — you could not price vegetables with it.

The rescue is a trick worth remembering, because it is pure Week-2 thinking. NVFP4 numbers never travel alone: they live in blocks of 16, and each block carries one shared scale factor — itself a small floating-point number (FP8, E4M3 anatomy) — with one full-precision FP32 scale over the whole tensor on top. Each little 4-bit value means “my sixteenth of the pattern, times my block’s scale, times the tensor’s scale.” The costume didn’t get smarter; the convention around it did — two levels of shared context squeeze meaning through a four-bit needle. It is positional notation’s own trick, one storey up: digits are cheap because the position carries the magnitude; here, values are cheap because the block carries it.

Why go through these contortions? Because at AI scale, bits are physics. A model with a trillion parameters stored in FP32 is four terabytes that must be fetched, again and again, through memory systems that burn energy per byte moved. Cut each number from 32 bits to 4 (plus a sliver for shared scales) and you have cut memory, bandwidth, and much of the energy bill by nearly 8× — which is the difference between an AI that runs in a datacenter and one that runs on your phone.

Three take-homes, all of them this course in miniature. Representation is a design choice, not a fact of nature — FP32 was never sacred, just convenient. The right representation depends on the reader — a bank reads every paisa; a neural network reads a pattern; giving both the same 32 bits wastes somebody’s silicon. And the frontier of computing in 2026 is being decided by exactly what you practised this week — exponents, fractions, biases, and the art of meaning more with fewer bits. The people designing whatever comes after NVFP4 are doing your Week 2 homework, for very high stakes.

A dose of curiosity: the five missing table entries that cost $475 million

In 1994, Thomas Nicely, a mathematics professor at a small college in Virginia, was doing something gloriously useless: computing Brun’s constant — the sum of the reciprocals of twin primes — to more digits than anyone had before. Pure curiosity; no product, no deadline. In June, he noticed his freshly assembled Pentium machine producing totals that disagreed, subtly, with his older machines and with published values.

Here is the part to study: he did not shrug. Almost anyone would have — blamed a library, a compiler, cosmic rays, themselves. Nicely spent months eliminating suspects one by one: his own code, the compiler, the operating system, the motherboard — until the only suspect left standing was the processor itself. In October 1994 he wrote to Intel; the young Internet did the rest.

The root cause is pure Week 2. The Pentium’s division hardware used a fast algorithm (SRT division) that looks up quotient digits in a table etched into the silicon — 1,066 entries. Five of them were missing — they read 0 where they should have read 2. Five wrong cells out of a thousand: divisions came out wrong only rarely, and when they did, the error typically appeared from about the fourth significant digit — the famous example being 4,195,835 ÷ 3,145,727, which the flawed chip got wrong where every ₹30 calculator got it right. Intel had noticed and quietly fixed new silicon; publicly it argued a typical user might hit the bug once in 9 billion divisions, and offered replacements only to users who could prove they needed accurate division. The Internet took that about as well as you’d expect. IBM halted Pentium shipments; in December Intel capitulated to a no-questions-asked recall — the first CPU recall in history — and wrote off $475 million.

Why does this belong in a week about number representation? Three reasons. First, representation is physical: your FP32 formula is not chalkboard decoration — it is implemented by actual transistors, and five absent ones silently corrupted arithmetic on millions of machines. Second, approximation errors do not stay where they are born: an error in the fourth digit of one division, fed into the next calculation and the next, compounds — in a long scientific computation, a spreadsheet of finances, or a structural simulation, a “tiny” representation error can explode into a wrong bridge load or a wrong drug dose. “It’s only the fourth digit” is not a sentence an engineer gets to say. And third — the temperament lesson this column keeps returning to — the bug was not found by Intel’s test suites. It was found by one curious person who checked his numbers against independent references and refused to shrug at a discrepancy in the ninth decimal place. The manufacturer had every resource; the professor had the audit habit. The habit won.

Thirty years later, a hobbyist put the actual flawed chip under a microscope and photographed the five missing cells in the silicon (Ken Shirriff’s analysis) — curiosity, again, closing the loop. Your 0.1 + 0.2 experiment this week is the same activity at student scale: don’t trust, don’t shrug, interrogate the representation.

Sources: Pentium FDIV bug — Wikipedia · Tom’s Hardware retrospective (30 years) · Ken Shirriff’s silicon-level analysis.
